# Journal Entry 003: Product vs. Feature Thinking — The Most Important PM Distinction

**Author:** Karani Njoroge
**Date:** June 2026
**Category:** Product Thinking
**Source:** Melissa Perri — Escaping the Build Trap + personal reflection
**Reading Time:** 5 minutes

---

## What I Learned

Feature thinking asks: "What should we build?" Product thinking asks: "What outcome are we trying to create, and is building the right way to get there?"

The build trap — Melissa Perri's term — is what happens when organizations measure success by output (features shipped, releases made) instead of outcomes (problems solved, value created). Teams in the build trap are busy and productive. They are also frequently building things nobody needs.

The distinction between a feature factory and a product company is this: feature factories take requests and build them. Product companies take problems and solve them — sometimes by building, sometimes not.

---

## Why It Matters for PMs

Every PM receives feature requests. From stakeholders, from users, from leadership. The PM's job is not to be a feature request router. It is to ask: "What outcome is this request in service of? Is this the best way to achieve that outcome?"

This is harder than it sounds. Stakeholders do not come with outcomes — they come with solutions. "Can we add a filter?" "Can we build a dashboard?" "Can we integrate with X?" The PM has to reverse-engineer the outcome the person actually needs, then evaluate whether their proposed solution is the best way to get there.

---

## Key Concepts

**Output:** What the team ships. Features, releases, code. Measurable but not inherently valuable.

**Outcome:** The change in user behavior or business performance that the output creates. This is what matters.

**The Build Trap:** When a team measures success by output rather than outcomes. Common in large organizations with annual roadmaps and quarterly shipping targets.

**Product Thinking:** Starting from the outcome and working backward to the solution — which may or may not be a new feature.

---

## Example from My Own Work

When I first scoped the hospital queue platform, my instinct was to add as many features as possible to make the PRD look comprehensive. Appointment scheduling, AI wait prediction, facility ratings, multi-hospital management. Each was a feature. None of them was an outcome.

When I forced myself to ask "what outcome are we trying to create?" — reduce patient abandonment — the feature list collapsed to four. Everything else was interesting but not proven to affect the outcome. Product thinking shrinks scopes and improves them simultaneously.
